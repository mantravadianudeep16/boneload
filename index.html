      Did today's workout protect your bones?

      BoneLoad turns the phone strapped to an astronaut's knee into a bone loading monitor. It tells the crew through the day whether their movement loaded the skeleton enough, and what to do if it didn't.

      Exercise only protects bone if it loads it hard enough

      Our challenge, Create Health Monitoring Software for Astronauts on Space Missions, names bone density loss as one of the hazards crews must spot and act on themselves.

      Bone needs force, not minutes

      Bone responds to peak force and how fast it rises. A few hard, fast reps beat many easy ones, but nobody tells astronauts which kind they just did.

      The Moon makes it worse

      At 1.62 m/s², walking on the lunar surface loads the skeleton about 1/6 as much as on Earth. Crews will need targeted exercise to make up the gap.

      Houston is far away

      With communication delays, the crew needs the answer on board, right after the workout, with clear next steps.

      How it works

      An accelerometer feels every force except gravity, so a phone on the leg feels every impact the skeleton takes.

      01 · Sense

      One phone on the knee

      The phone's accelerometer and gyroscope are read in the browser at about 60 samples per second. Nothing leaves the phone. A 3 second stand-still check confirms the sensor and strap.

      02 · Compute

      A model, then a score

      The signal is averaged in 0.25 second steps that keep their peaks. A k-nearest-neighbours model reads 2 seconds of them, names the activity, and is smoothed so it does not flicker. Impact peaks and squat time turn into two daily scores.

      score = 100 · (1 − e−dose/D)
      03 · Act

      A clear next step

      A status of Protected, Watch or At risk, with advice such as “about 70 more hops or heel drops” or “3 more minutes of squats”, and alerts for hard impacts and long stretches with no strong impact.

      Live simulation

      Watch slow squats barely move the score, then jump squats fill it. Switch to the Moon to see the same workout lose most of its load. Simulated data, same physics as our analysis code. This is the full three-sensor concept (pelvis, knee, ankle) the team is building toward. The Monitor tab runs the one-phone version.

      What NASA's bone data says

      • MR035G DXA scans, Campaign 3 bed rest: hip bone density fell 2.59% on average when load was removed (17 subjects with hip scans).
      • MR079G strength change vs hip bone change: ρ = 0.41, p = 0.10. A faint trend, not proven. Spine and heel showed nothing.
      • NASA's public archive has no exercise-dose or impact records, so the loading targets here are heuristics. ARED, Force_Shoes and vertical-jump records would calibrate them.

      Hardware

      One pod design on an adjustable velcro strap fits almost anyone. Accelerometer and phone modules share the same dovetail clip, or stick straight onto the strap with velcro.

      Phone-only today

      The monitor needs one phone strapped just above the kneecap. A phone is heavier than a sensor pod, so pull the strap extra tight or it records fake impacts.

      Calibrate once, wear one

      In the full system a weekly 5-minute session with all three sensors teaches the app each person's leg. Every other day, the pelvis sensor alone predicts the rest.

      Accelerometers only

      Force, femur load, impacts, jump height and balance need no gyroscope. Gyros later unlock fast joint angles and neck torques.

      Flight version

      In orbit, slow resistance exercise barely accelerates the body, so the flight design adds in-line load cells on the treadmill harness.

      What we measure today, and what comes next

      We measure the loading that goes into the skeleton. Stress inside bone needs scans and musculoskeletal models, so we don't claim it.

      1. Knee phone acceleration and on-device activity model: built
      2. Daily impact and resistance loading score with advice: built, with heuristic targets
      3. Ground force and bone loading score from the pelvis: built in the simulation
      4. Femur and knee loads from three sensors: in progress
      5. Targets calibrated on NASA exercise-dose records (ARED, Force_Shoes), which need a NASA data request
      6. Neck module: astronauts had cervical disc herniations at 21.4 times the control rate, cause unknown. Needs a head sensor with a gyroscope.
      7. Muscle and joint contact forces (musculoskeletal modeling)
      8. Stress inside bone (needs CT or MRI geometry)
